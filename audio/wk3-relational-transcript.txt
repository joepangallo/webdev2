Jordan: Be honest with me. MongoDB is the modern one and MySQL is the old one you put up with until you can upgrade, right? A document is basically a table row with the rules taken off.
Alex: Neither one is the upgrade of the other. Picture a library that keeps one numbered card per author. The name is written on that one card only, and every book gets a slip that just says which card number to look at. Now picture the other system: no card box at all, just one folder per book, with everything about that book — title, author, extras — written inside its own folder. The card box is the relational picture. The folder is the document picture. Two ways of filing the same facts, not two generations of the same tool.
Jordan: Okay, so where does that actually bite? It is the same books either way.
Alex: Take the section's own example: three authors and three books. In the tables, the authors table holds the names — Frank Herbert in row one, Jane Austen in row two, J.R.R. Tolkien in row three. The books table never repeats a name. Emma's row holds the number two — the id of Jane Austen's row. One query can join Emma to Jane Austen by author ID. In the document example, the author's name is already inside the book. Now watch what that buys you. Suppose Jane Austen's name was spelled wrong and needs fixing. You edit one row, once, and every book that points at it is instantly correct, because the name itself is stored exactly once.
Jordan: And in the folder version?
Alex: The Dune document carries everything inside itself: the title, and the author written out in full, Frank Herbert, right there in the document. And it carries things a table would struggle to hold: a list of genres, Science fiction and Adventure, in one field, and the editions nested inside — the paperback at 688 pages, the ebook in EPUB format. Ask for the book and you get the whole bundle in one step, no pointer to chase. But here is the cost. Frank Herbert's name is now copied into every one of his book documents. Change it, and you must find and fix every copy. Miss one, and your own database disagrees with itself about who wrote what.
Jordan: So the card box makes you chase a pointer when you read, and the folder makes you chase copies when you update.
Alex: Exactly. And there is a second difference: who enforces the shape. The table declares its plan in advance, and the database rejects a book with no title, because the table declared the title required. This collection, by default, has no such gatekeeper — no validator requiring a title. One book document can carry a subtitle and the next one need not — that is allowed on purpose.
Jordan: Then no schema means no rules at all. Anything goes, and sooner or later the data is soup.
Alex: That is the first mix-up, the no-rules mix-up. No schema in the database does not mean no rules — it means the rules move into your code. The database will happily store a book with no title unless your program checks. You traded enforcement for flexibility; you did not delete the need for care.
Jordan: Copying the author's name into every document — that is just sloppy duplication, isn't it? Something a beginner does before learning tables.
Alex: That is the second mix-up, the sloppy-duplication mix-up. It is a deliberate trade, not a mistake — the copies are the price of reading in one step. The section's matching examples make the point: library loans go relational because loans tie books, members, and due dates together in shared relationships, and concert seat sales belong in tables because two buyers must never book the same seat — that is consistency, not update volume — while the recipe collection and the photo app's posts go in documents.
