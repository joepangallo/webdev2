Jordan: Okay, so once someone is logged in, they are trusted — security handled. After that, any route is fair game. Right?
Alex: That is the classic mistake. Logging in answers one question: who are you? That is authentication. Authorization is the second question, asked on every route that matters: now that I know who you are, may you do this thing? Picture a hotel key card. Checking in proves who you are — that gets you the card. But the card is programmed as a guest card, not a staff card. Try a staff-floor door and the lock reads the card, knows exactly whose it is, and still refuses to open. One card, two questions — and the second gets asked at every door.
Jordan: So every route is a door with its own lock?
Alex: Every route that matters, yes. And the locks are set from one document: the permission matrix. Actions down one side, roles across the top — student, instructor, admin — and a yes or no in every cell. All three roles can view course pages, submit homework, and view their own grades. Instructors and admins enter and edit grades; only admins delete a course or manage user accounts. Every guard you write is one cell of the table turned into code.
Jordan: And the guard is the lock?
Alex: Exactly — and it is middleware, from Week 2: it runs before your handler and can stop the request. The section builds two. The first asks only the identity question: no signed-in user, and it answers four-oh-one, "Please log in first." The second takes a role — each account has one, copied into the session at login. It checks identity first — nobody logged in still gets four-oh-one — and only then compares the session's role with the role the route demands. Wrong role, and it answers four-oh-three: "You are not allowed to do this." Identity first, permission second, because you cannot decide what someone may do until you know who they are. And the match is exact: the grades guard accepts instructor only, so an admin session gets the same four-oh-three from it — "equals this role," not "this role or higher."
Jordan: Walk me through the two traces, then — the two different kinds of no.
Alex: First request: a visitor with no session cookie sends a GET to slash my-grades. There is no identity to check, so the guard stops it: four-oh-one, "Please log in first." On the client, the usual move is to show a login page. Second request: a signed-in student sends a POST to slash grades, a route that demands the instructor role. This time authentication passes — the server knows who this is — and authorization fails. Four-oh-three, "You are not allowed to do this."
Jordan: All right, first mix-up, then. If the page never shows a student the Enter grades button, the route behind it is safe — they cannot click what they cannot see.
Alex: That is the hidden-button mix-up. Hiding the button is good design; it is not security. Remember sending requests with curl in Weeks 1 and 2? A student can send that same POST without ever opening the page. Real apps hide the button and enforce the rule on the server. The guard is the lock.
Jordan: Second one. When the student hits the instructor route, I would have answered four-oh-one — four-oh-one literally says Unauthorized.
Alex: That is the wrong-no mix-up, and the name is the trap. Despite its name, four-oh-one is about authentication; it means, "You have not proven who you are." It belongs to the visitor with no cookie, the request with the invalid token — cases where the server cannot start the permission question. Four-oh-three, Forbidden, is the permission answer — the server saying, "I know exactly who you are, and you still may not do this." A signed-in student posting grades is the clearest four-oh-three there is. Get the two backwards and your app tells logged-in users to log in, and tells anonymous visitors they are forbidden.
Jordan: And the rule for filling the matrix in?
Alex: Least privilege: every role gets the smallest permission set it needs. Start every cell at no, and make each yes earn its place by naming the task that needs it. A student role that can also edit grades "just in case" is not convenience — it is a bug waiting for a curious student.
Jordan: Got it. Test me.

Jordan: Four-oh-three. The server knows exactly who she is, so it is not a login problem — deleting courses is admin-only, and the guard refuses her.
